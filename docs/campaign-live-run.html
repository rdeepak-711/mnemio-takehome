How the replies of the run were read

Why "needs review" instead of a guess?

When the AI isn't sure, or isn't reachable, a reply becomes needs_review. That stops the prospect's emails just as "not interested" would, and waits for a person. A wrong "interested" sends a salesperson after someone who said no; a wrong "out of office" keeps emailing someone who asked to stop. Stopping is the safe way to fail.

In this run the AI never put a reply in the wrong bucket: every one of its misses is a "needs review" (see the table).

How this was made, and what guarantees it

A frozen export of one run

npm run full-run on 25 Oct 2026, on a scratch copy of the database: 10,000 seeded prospects (SEED=42), deliverability and hourly pacing on, a live OpenRouter key on the free models. It reset, seeded, had the AI write the 20 sequences, advanced the simulated clock until nobody was left in the sequence, and checked itself.

The numbers were read back with the same functions the dashboard uses (summaryAt, timeline, movesOn, deliveryDay, classifierStats) and pasted into this file, so it opens from disk and connects to nothing.

A mock email provider, deterministic

No email was ever sent. src/mock/provider.ts stands in for a service like SendGrid, on its own database connection. It decides every outcome from a hash of the seed and the email's key (contact_id:step): 3% of addresses bounce, 8% of accepted emails get a reply 12–72 hours later (30% interested, 35% not interested, 20% out of office, 15% unsubscribe), and some replies to the last email arrive after the prospect was deactivated. A re-run gives the same outcomes; the AI's text and labels are the only live part.

Why nobody gets an email twice

  1. Workers claim due prospects with FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED, so two never hold the same person.
  2. A batch's sends, records and status changes commit as one transaction.
  3. The provider remembers each contact_id:step key and answers a retry without sending again.
  4. UNIQUE (contact_id, step) on sends refuses a second row.

At the end, checkInvariants checked 13 rules across every row (for example: one send per person per step, no mailbox over its daily or hourly limit, no email outside working hours) and printed invariants: ok.
